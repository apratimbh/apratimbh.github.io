But today’s public interaction model is still largely framed as a user-initiated conversation: you “Go Live,” speak, and get spoken responses. That is still different from a truly always-on assistant that decides for itself when to intervene.

In real-world assistance, success depends not just on what to say, but also on whether the system should say anything at all.

03

Streaming benchmarks are advancing fast

Academic work has been moving quickly as well. Two especially useful trackers are Awesome Streaming Video Understanding and Awesome Streaming LLMs, which curate models, methods, datasets, and taxonomies for this rapidly growing area.

At the benchmark level, three influential recent efforts are StreamingBench, OVO-Bench, and RIVER. Together, these benchmarks move beyond offline video QA toward temporally grounded, streaming interaction.

StreamingBench

Real-time visual understanding, omni-source understanding, contextual understanding.

OVO-Bench

Timestamp-aware online video reasoning across past, present, and future.

RIVER

Retrospective memory, live perception, and proactive response in streaming dialogue.

04

What these benchmarks actually test

Current

Understanding current events

The ability to interpret what is happening right now from the current stream. In OVO-Bench, this appears as Real-Time Visual Perception.

Past

Recalling past events

The ability to recover information that is no longer visible. OVO-Bench’s Backward Tracing makes this concrete with examples like “What did I drop in the bowl in the sink?”

Future

Tracking future events

The ability to keep monitoring until a relevant event actually happens. OVO-Bench’s Forward Active Responding captures this.

Timing

Knowing “when to say”

The ability to decide whether to answer now or wait for more evidence. OVO-Bench’s Clues Reveal Responding is the clearest example.

05

Why current benchmarks are not enough

These benchmark capabilities are important — but they are still often evaluated in relative isolation. Real-world tasks usually require several of them at once: understanding the present, remembering past progress, anticipating what comes next, detecting when something has gone wrong, and deciding whether to interrupt the user.

Recent work reinforces how much room there still is to improve evaluation. In particular, SimpleStream showed that an off-the-shelf VLM using only a tiny sliding window of recent frames can already match or beat many specialized streaming systems. That suggests current benchmarks are useful, but still not yet sufficient stress tests for richer, real-world multimodal assistance.

06

Interactive task guidance: a stronger and more realistic testbed

A particularly compelling next step is interactive task guidance: benchmarks where an AI system must guide a person through a real-world task such as a fitness routine or a recipe, providing instructions, checking progress, and intervening when mistakes occur.

This setup naturally combines perception, memory, anticipation, temporal grounding, and proactive communication in one unified problem. It is much closer to what future assistants — and many embodied systems — will actually need to do.

This is the motivation behind QEVD / Live Fitness Coaching (NeurIPS 2024), which introduced fitness coaching as a benchmark for situated interaction. The key challenge is not just understanding human motion, but recognizing mistakes and delivering the right feedback at the right time, asynchronously, as the activity unfolds.

That idea continues in Qualcomm Interactive Cooking (NeurIPS 2025), which asks whether multimodal LLMs can provide live step-by-step task guidance. The benchmark requires models not only to provide the next instruction, but also to detect successful completion, identify mistakes, and issue corrective feedback at precisely the right moment.

08

Why this matters

Guiding a person through a real-world task is one of the cleanest testbeds we have for multimodal intelligence under continuous input. It compresses a large fraction of the real problem into a single scenario: the model must interpret visual scenes, track task progress, remember earlier actions, anticipate next steps, detect mistakes, and communicate proactively — all while the world keeps moving.

That makes interactive task guidance a powerful benchmark not only for AI assistants, but also for the capabilities we eventually want in embodied agents and household robots. The nice part is that it disentangles thinking from acting: the model provides the intelligence and guidance, while the human remains the action expert executing the task.

Can multimodal models move beyond passive description and become timely, context-aware collaborators in the physical world?

The answer today is still “not yet” — but these benchmarks are showing us exactly what is missing, and what we need to build next.

Interactive Task Guidance as a Testbed for Multimodal Intelligence

From turn-based multimodal chat to real-world, always-on assistance.

Core claim: Real-world multimodal intelligence is not just about understanding images, video, or speech — it is about deciding when to act and what to say as a continuous stream unfolds.

State-of-the-art multimodal LLMs are impressive — but most of them still operate in a fundamentally turn-based way. They respond when we ask. They wait when we do not. That works well for many text- and image-based tasks, but it is not how intelligence works in the real world. Biological agents do not wait for a prompt before integrating vision, memory, and action. They continuously perceive, interpret, and adapt.

If we want AI systems that behave more like real assistants — and eventually support more general-purpose robotics — they need to reason over continuous multimodal streams, not just isolated prompts.
